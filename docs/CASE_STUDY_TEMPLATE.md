# Customer case-study template — reference

This is the documented reference for `templates/case-study.md`. It
explains each section, why the section matters, and how to fill it
in well. Use the two files together: edit `templates/case-study.md`
when you are drafting a new case study, and update this file when
the structure of the template changes.

If you add, remove, or rename a section, update **both** files so
they stay in sync.

---

## Overview

The template is structured around a consistent narrative arc:

1. Clinic size and specialty (anonymized) — who they are.
2. What they were missing — the pain.
3. What Zorva found — the catches.
4. Dollars recovered — the financial outcome.
5. Time saved — the operational outcome.
6. What the biller says — the human voice.

The arc moves from context to problem to evidence to outcome to
testimonial. It is the structure a clinic administrator, a billing
manager, or a procurement lead can scan in under two minutes and
still walk away with the key numbers.

---

## Section 1 — Clinic size and specialty (anonymized)

**Why it matters:** The reader is matching the case study to their
own practice. They need to know whether the clinic is "like me"
(same size, same specialty, same region, same payer mix) before
they trust the outcome.

**How to fill it:** One short paragraph. Cover four fields: clinic
size, specialty, region, payer mix. Anonymize the clinic name and
the provider names. Keep it factual, not flattering.

## Section 2 — What they were missing

**Why it matters:** This is the problem statement. It frames the
case study in the reader's own language. A clinic administrator
reading "we suspected under-coded E/M visits" thinks "we suspect
that too" — and keeps reading.

**How to fill it:** One paragraph. State the operational or
financial pain the clinic was experiencing before the pilot.
Mention the metric they were tracking (denial rate, days-in-A/R,
under-coding rate) and what it looked like. Do not promise a
specific outcome here — that comes in sections 4 and 5.

## Section 3 — What Zorva found

**Why it matters:** This is the evidence section. The reader needs
to see Zorva in action, not just a number at the end. Concrete
findings, with the rule name and the encounter context, build the
trust that a single dollar figure cannot.

**How to fill it:** One paragraph. List the top 2–3 rule
categories the auditor fired on. Use the actual rule names (e.g.
`em_level_upcode`, `modifier_unlock_25`, `unbilled_procedure`).
Quote the exact wording the auditor used so the reader can see
the catch in their own words. Keep it tight — two or three
categories is enough; this is not a feature list.

## Section 4 — Dollars recovered

**Why it matters:** This is the headline number. For most readers
it is the first thing they will look for, and the only number
they will remember.

**How to fill it:** Two fields. **Time window / measurement
period** is required — always say which 60 or 90 days the number
covers and what the baseline was. **Total** is the dollar figure
or the percent of revenue. The optional **breakdown** shows the
contribution of each rule category, which makes the number
defensible to a billing manager who asks "where did that come
from?".

If the clinic prefers a range over a specific number, use a
banded figure ("$15K–$20K recovered") and say so explicitly.

## Section 5 — Time saved

**Why it matters:** Dollars recovered tells the CFO yes. Time
saved tells the biller yes. A case study that has only the dollar
number undersells the operational win.

**How to fill it:** Same two-field structure as section 4. State
the **time window** (per week, per month, across the pilot) and
the **total** (hours plus the FTE equivalent). State the
**measurement method** — self-reported in the weekly review,
cross-checked against the audit-trail accept / dismiss timestamps,
or both. The reader needs to be able to trust the number, and
"six hours" without a measurement method does not survive a
procurement review.

## Section 6 — What the biller says

**Why it matters:** A direct quote from the clinic's biller carries
more weight than a paragraph of marketing copy. The biller is the
person who actually used the product and the person the reader
trusts.

**How to fill it:** One short direct quote, attributed to
"[Name], [Title], [Anonymized Clinic]". Keep it specific to the
clinic's situation (what changed for them), not generic praise of
Zorva. Get written permission from the named biller before
publishing. The signed quote release lives in the customer file.

## Anonymization rules

- **Clinic name:** never use the real clinic name in the published
  case study. Use a generic descriptor (region + specialty) or
  "[Anonymized Clinic]".
- **Provider names:** first name and last initial (e.g. "Dr. Maria
  L.") or a role title.
- **Patient identifiers:** none. The auditor works on hashed
  patient IDs, so the source data should not contain real names,
  PHNs, or DOBs in the first place.
- **Real dollar figures:** allowed. The number is the point. Use a
  banded range only if the clinic asks for one.
- **Direct quotes:** get written permission from the named biller
  or clinic contact before publishing. The signed release lives in
  the customer file.

---

## Out of scope for the template

- Visual layout, brand styling, or export formats (PDF, web,
  print). Design is handled by marketing once the draft is signed
  off.
- Methodology definitions, KPI dictionaries, or product-feature
  inventories. Keep the case study focused on this one clinic.
- A second case study, a comparison table, or a side-by-side with
  competitors. One clinic, one story.
